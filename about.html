Get to know me

About

I came to software through a laboratory, and I never really left the habit of testing things.

(01) — The long way round

The first thing I ever tried to optimise was a germination test. I was an intern at the Institute of Agricultural Research and Training in Ibadan, running tissue culture and sterilisation work under GLP guidelines, and applying statistics to plant breeding data. Bench work teaches you a particular discipline: you do not get to argue with the result. Either the seeds germinated or they did not.

I studied biological sciences, and I expected to stay in that world. What pulled me out was noticing how much of the work was already computational — how much of the laboratory was really data, and how much of the bottleneck was tools that nobody had built yet.

So I started building them. First HTML and CSS in 2022, then JavaScript, then React and Next.js, then Dart and Flutter. I have been shipping continuously since — first frontend practice, then real client work, then production platforms, and most recently smart contracts.

I do not think of the science degree as a detour. It is the reason I ask what could go wrong before I ask what could go right, and the reason I am comfortable being wrong early, when it is cheap.

(02) — How I work

Four things I believe about this job

Working
principles

01 — Performance is a feature, not a phase

Most of the people who use what I build are on mid-range Android phones with intermittent data. That constraint is not an edge case to be handled later; it is the actual design brief. It shapes what I reach for before I write a line of code.

02 — The unglamorous parts are the product

Empty states, error states, the moment the network drops, what happens when a form is submitted twice. These are the parts users actually experience, and they are usually the parts nobody put in the ticket. I treat them as first-class work.

03 — Handover is part of shipping

A platform is not finished when it works for me. It is finished when the person who owns the content can update it without calling a developer, and when the next engineer can read the code and the documentation and know why it is the way it is.

04 — Be precise about what is done

I would rather write down a limitation than let someone discover it in production. Several of the projects on this site list what they still need, because that is the honest state of the work.
