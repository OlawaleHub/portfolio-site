Client Portfolio Builds

01

The brief

A COO and marketing strategist needed a professional web presence — somewhere to send recruiters and conference contacts that was not a PDF attachment or a LinkedIn profile.

The requirements were refreshingly practical: it should load quickly, look credible on a phone, be easy to point a domain at, and not require a monthly bill or a maintenance relationship afterwards. This is the kind of brief where the right answer is usually the smallest thing that works.

I delivered three iterations across the same engagement as the content and positioning were refined.

02

Why static was the right call

For a portfolio site with a handful of pages and content that changes a few times a year, a database and a server are pure overhead. Every one of them is something that can go down, cost money, or need a security patch.

Static files on a CDN sidestep all of it. The pages are pre-built, the hosting is effectively free at this scale, the attack surface is close to nil, and there is no server to keep awake. It is the rare case where the technically simplest option is also the fastest and the cheapest.

One of the builds went further and used Next.js where the layout benefited from a component structure; the others are straightforward HTML and CSS. Choosing per project rather than by habit is the point.

03

Designed at 375 pixels first

The primary device for this audience is a phone — someone opening a link from an email or a WhatsApp message while commuting. Designing at desktop width and then squeezing it down produces exactly the wrong result: text that is too small, navigation that collapses badly, and images that dominate the first screen.

So every layout starts at the narrow breakpoint. Type sizes, tap targets and spacing are chosen there, then allowed to breathe as the viewport grows. The desktop version is the enhancement, not the origin.

That approach also enforces discipline about content. If a section cannot justify its space on a 375-pixel screen, it usually cannot justify it on a 1440-pixel one either.

04

Handover and ownership

The most important design decision on a project like this is what happens after I stop working on it. A portfolio that only its developer can update is a liability, not an asset — the client ends up emailing me to change a job title.

So the content lives in plain, readable markup with clear section boundaries, and I walked the client through editing it directly. Where a change was likely to be recurring, I optimised for that specifically rather than for elegance in the abstract.

Each site was deployed to a live domain with HTTPS and a build that reruns automatically on push, so publishing is a matter of editing a file.

05

What I would do differently

Three iterations of the same site is two more than necessary, and the reason was process rather than craft: I started building before the content and positioning were settled, so each round of copy changes became a round of layout changes.

The fix is not more design tooling. It is refusing to start until the actual text exists, because real sentences break layouts in ways placeholder text never does. Placeholder content flatters every design.

I would also add analytics and a contact-form fallback from day one. Without a way to see whether visitors reach the contact page, both the client and I are guessing about which sections are earning their place — and guessing is what you do when you did not instrument the thing.
