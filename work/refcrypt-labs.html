01

The brief

Refcrypt Labs is a software and blockchain studio in Lekki, Lagos, founded in 2021 out of Reffinance. It works across mobile and web development, Web3 integration, smart contracts, data and AI, and the marketing services that surround them — nine service lines in total.

The website has to do something awkward and common: present a broad, technical practice to non-technical buyers, while remaining credible to engineers who will read it. It also has to be maintainable by a small team that is busy with client delivery, which is the constraint that actually drives most of the decisions.

I am on the web and mobile team. I build and maintain this platform alongside client products, and the source is confidential, so what follows is the approach rather than the code.

02

Architecture and the marketing surface

The platform is a Next.js application with Tailwind for styling, deployed on Vercel. Public pages are rendered so that they arrive fast and are indexable, which matters because a services business with nine offerings lives or dies on search visibility for each of them.

A headless CMS sits behind it. Content — service descriptions, team, products, blog posts — is structured rather than hard-coded, which is what makes the site maintainable by people who are not me. Adding a service line should be a content operation, not a deployment.

The site presents four named products in the studio's orbit and a set of client logos. One deliberate rule I hold to: only publish client names that have been cleared for publication. Unnamed logos are preferable to a claim a client has not agreed to.

03

SEO as a structural concern

For a services studio, organic search is the primary acquisition channel, so SEO is not a plugin bolted on at the end — it decides the URL structure, the heading hierarchy and the page templates.

In practice that means: one page per service with genuinely distinct content rather than a single features page; semantic headings that reflect the actual content hierarchy; metadata, Open Graph tags and canonical URLs generated per route; and fast pages, because Core Web Vitals are a ranking input as well as a user-experience one.

It also means resisting the temptation to render everything client-side. Content that search engines and social previews need to read should be in the delivered HTML.

04

Maintenance is the real deliverable

A marketing site is never finished. Service lines change, products launch, the team shifts, copy gets rewritten by people who are better at copy than I am.

So I optimised for the steady state rather than the launch: content that the team can edit, a component structure where a new page is composition rather than invention, and no bespoke mechanism that only one person understands.

The measure of success is not how the site looked on delivery day. It is that months later, the team is still publishing on it without needing to schedule my time.

05

What I would do differently

The honest note on this project is about tidiness at the edges. A site that is edited continuously accumulates small defects — a dead link in the navigation, placeholder anchors in a footer, a copyright year that stops being updated. They are individually trivial and collectively the difference between a site that feels maintained and one that feels abandoned.

What I would add is a lightweight automated check that runs on every deploy: link validation across the built output, a lint for placeholder or empty hrefs, and a failing build if either appears. Running it in CI means the team cannot reintroduce the problem without noticing, which is more reliable than me remembering to look.

That is the general lesson I keep relearning: quality that depends on someone remembering is not quality, it is luck with a maintenance schedule.
