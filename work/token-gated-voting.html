Token-Gated Voting

Governance where influence costs something, and cancelling a vote gives the money back.

01

The brief

Most token governance has a participation problem that is easier to see than to solve. Holding a token is free, so voting costs nothing, so the people who show up are the ones with the strongest opinion rather than the strongest stake — and a determined actor can borrow a large position, vote, and return it in the same transaction.

The premise of this capstone was to make influence cost something. A voter pays a fixed amount of an ERC-20 token to cast a vote, and gets it back if the proposal is cancelled. Economic alignment, rather than pure token weighting.

It was built as a four-person group capstone over the WEB3 programme, and I wrote the governor extension and its test suite.

02

Building on Governor rather than from scratch

Governance is a domain where inventing your own mechanics is a bad instinct. Vote counting, proposal lifecycle, timelocks and quorum all have well-understood failure modes that a battle-tested implementation has already met.

So the contract extends OpenZeppelin's Governor, composing the modules it needs: proposal settings, simple counting, token voting, quorum as a fraction of supply, timelock control, role-based access control and a pause switch. That is eight modules assembled into one governor rather than a bespoke voting contract.

The custom behaviour sits on top as PaidGovernor: the payment, the refund, and the admin surface.

03

How paid voting works

castVoteWithPayment is the primary entry point, and it is guarded carefully:

  • It is disabled when the contract is paused.
  • It requires a non-zero vote cost and that the address has not already paid for this proposal — one paid vote per address.
  • It transfers the cost from the voter to a configured treasury, or to a burn address when burning is switched on.
  • It then records the vote, forcing a minimum weight of one so a payment always counts for something.

The standard castVote and castVoteWithReason remain available only while free voting is enabled. Once the admin turns free voting off, they revert with a message pointing at the paid path, so there is no quiet way to bypass the gate.

04

Why snapshotting matters more than the gate

The paid gate makes voting costly for a casual attacker. The snapshot is what makes borrowed-vote attacks structurally impossible.

Vote weight is read from getVotes(voter, block.number - 1) — a past block, not the present. A voter therefore has to hold the tokens before the proposal is raised, not merely at the moment of voting, which removes the entire flash-loan class of attack regardless of how much capital is available.

That is why the contract uses ERC20Votes rather than a plain ERC-20 as its voting source. The gate and the snapshot solve different problems, and only together do they mean anything.

05

Refunds and the treasury

If a proposal is cancelled, the people who paid to vote on it should not be out of pocket. Cancellation is admin-only and restricted to proposals that are still pending or active — once a proposal has been executed or defeated, the money has already served its purpose.

claimRefund requires that the proposal is cancelled, that the caller actually paid, and that they have not already claimed. It returns exactly the cost, and it is idempotent by construction: the hasRefunded flag is set before the transfer.

The destination of the money is a first-class configuration choice. With burnInsteadOfTreasury on, vote payments are destroyed rather than accumulated, which is a deflationary stance a DAO might reasonably prefer. With it off, the treasury accrues and the governor can be funded by its own governance.

06

What I would do differently

The most valuable part of this project was not the happy path, it was the review. Two things in the committed contract are worth stating plainly, because leaving them unsaid would misrepresent the state of the work:

  • The GovernorSettings constructor receives four positional arguments where the OpenZeppelin version takes three, and the inline comments do not line up with the parameter positions. That is the kind of defect that only a compile and a test run will reliably surface, and it should be corrected and recompiled.
  • The contract imports Pausable from the security path while the pinned OpenZeppelin version is 5.x, which moved it under utils. Another compile-level break that needs fixing before this can be described as buildable.

I am listing these rather than quietly regenerating the repository because the honest version is more instructive. The mechanics are sound and the test suite exists; the build needs a pass, and I would not claim otherwise.

Beyond the fixes, the next real step is deployment and a DAO that actually uses it. A governance contract with no community is a very well-tested hypothesis.
