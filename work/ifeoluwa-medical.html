A publishing platform where a doctor writes and publishes without ever calling a developer.

01

The brief

A medical professional wanted to publish health writing for the general public — practical, evidence-based, and deliberately free of the two failure modes that dominate health content online: impenetrable jargon and fear-mongering.

The requirement that shaped every technical decision was not visual. It was ownership. She needed to be able to write and publish on her own schedule without opening a code editor, and without a developer in the loop for routine updates.

The site carries a standing disclaimer on every page — that the content is informational and not medical advice — which is a content-governance decision as much as a legal one.

02

Choosing a headless CMS over a database

The obvious alternative was a conventional database with a small admin panel. I chose a headless CMS instead, and the deciding factor was what the author would actually experience.

Sanity gives a purpose-built editing environment for rich text and images out of the box, with a preview of structured content rather than a pile of HTML. Building an equivalent editor by hand would have taken longer and been worse.

The content model is deliberately small: an article with rich text, a cover image and an author reference; an author record; and Sanity's image pipeline for assets. Small models are easier for editors to reason about, and they do not accumulate dead fields.

03

The Studio lives inside the site

The single most useful decision was embedding Sanity Studio at /studio rather than hosting it separately. The author logs in on the same domain as the public site and gets the full editing interface there.

That removes an entire class of confusion. There is one URL to remember, one login, and no separation between “the website” and “the thing I write in”. From her side, writing an article is a form on a page she recognises.

04

Static pages, live content

Public pages are statically generated, which is what makes the site fast on a phone over a poor connection. The tension with static generation is freshness: how does a newly published article appear without a redeploy?

The answer is incremental static regeneration with a 60-second revalidation window. An article publishes in the Studio, and within a minute the public pages have rebuilt in the background. Readers get static-delivery speed; the author gets near-live publishing. Nobody has to trigger a deploy.

05

Details worth naming

  • Images are served through next/image from Sanity's CDN, with the remote pattern allow-listed in the Next config, so covers are resized and served in modern formats automatically.
  • Rich text renders through Portable Text, which means the published markup stays semantic rather than inheriting whatever the editor pasted in.
  • Article count on the about page is derived from the content itself, not hand-maintained, so it cannot drift out of date.
  • Typography and layout are kept deliberately plain: a medical-blue and white palette, comfortable measure, and no decorative elements competing with the writing.
06

What I would do differently

The honest limitation is volume: the site was built and handed over with a single published article, so it has been proven as a working platform but not yet stress-tested against a large back catalogue. Pagination on the article index, related-article logic, and search are the three things I would build next, and all three only meaningfully matter once there are dozens of posts rather than one.

I would also add structured data and an RSS feed from the start. Health writing is exactly the kind of content that benefits from being discoverable outside the site it lives on, and both are cheap to add early and annoying to retrofit.

What the project did prove is the part that mattered to the client: she can publish on her own, immediately, and the site stays fast.
